  body_markdown: "Frankly speaking, I think you have to read a whole lot more to get\
    \ a better idea of the problem.\n\nA couple links from amazon (in no particular\
    \ order: just easily spotted and cheap)\n[Introduction-Autonomous-Mobile-Intelligent-Robotics](http://www.amazon.com/Introduction-Autonomous-Mobile-Intelligent-Robotics/dp/0262015358/ref=sr_1_2?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1354008242&sr=1-2&keywords=slam+robotics)\n\
    \n[FastSLAM-Scalable-Simultaneous-Localization-Robotics](http://www.amazon.com/FastSLAM-Scalable-Simultaneous-Localization-Robotics/dp/3642079784/ref=sr_1_3?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1354008242&sr=1-3&keywords=slam+robotics)\n\
    \nNow onto your specific question.\nEvery entity that has no navigate an environment\
    \ has to accomplish multiple tasks:\n\n - path finding\n - obstacle avoidance\n\
    \ - map construction\n - localization\n\n\n***PATH FINDING***\n\nThere are a number\
    \ of algorithms (from the well known A* and D* to the most esoteric ones) but\
    \ these all depend on the way you represent the navigation map (which may be different\
    \ from say the real-time map, or the obstacle map: you may have different resolution\
    \ maps for different tasks).\n\nThe simplest theory is this: your map is a connected\
    \ graph, the algorithm tries to find the shortest path between two nodes. This\
    \ way the hardest part, the one that determines where a node effectively is, is\
    \ moved outside the path planning and into the map construction.\n\nThe key point\
    \ here being what and where is a node. The amount of nodes heavily depends on\
    \ the algorithm for the path planning: some algorithms have very short expansions\
    \ (meaning they wander very short distances from nodes, trying to stay near them)\
    \ so you need lots of nodes to cover the environment (grid maps are often used\
    \ here), other algorithms have much wider expansions, so you can have fewer nodes\
    \ representing the area (voronoi expansions and medial axis can be used when you\
    \ can wander far from nodes).\n\n\n***OBSTACLE AVOIDANCE***\n\nThe task of avoiding\
    \ obstacles may be complicated ad-libitum.\nMeaning you can go from bumpers (like\
    \ the first Roombas) up to multiple stereo-cameras and laser range finders (like\
    \ the autonomous cars developed by Google or for the [Darpa Urban Challenge](http://archive.darpa.mil/grandchallenge/)\
    \ <-check it out!)\n\nFor most applications a laser ranger (Hokuyo and SICK are\
    \ the most famous manufacturers) is the default choice: such a sensor gives you\
    \ a distance reading across a circular sector up to 270\xB0, with resolutions\
    \ up to 0.25\xB0, at frequencies up 40Hz and distances up 80 m.\n\nThe key point\
    \ though is how you process your data.\nIn fact you have to decide how you categorize\
    \ an obstacle -meaning how you determine what sequence of data is effectively\
    \ an obstacle, and what properties an obstacle can have- and after that how this\
    \ categorization affects the path planning and/or map building.\n\nThe simplest\
    \ (but still working) obstacle avoidance is [the VFH(+ or *) method](http://www-personal.umich.edu/~johannb/vff&vfh.htm):\
    \ this is the simplest method still doing some kind of data coalescence. Otherwise\
    \ an even simpler method would be: get as far away from the nearest obstacle.\n\
    \nMore sophisticated methods involve determining some characteristics of the obstacle\
    \ (like dimensions, shape, orientation) and placing it in the map, to allow the\
    \ planner to plan a path around the obstacle.\nBut again this depends on the kind\
    \ and resolution of the map: for example if your planning algorithm runs on a\
    \ low resolution grid map, you may even not put obstacles in it: an obstacle would\
    \ only be a temporary perturbation in the calculated trajectory, you go along\
    \ with the perturbation (the OA algorithm driving you away from the path) and\
    \ then you rejoin the path when the perturbation has ended.\n\nInstead if you\
    \ do 6D mapping and planning, everything goes in the map and the hardest part\
    \ becomes *Shape Detection* in the data cloud and registration.\n\n\n\n***MAP\
    \ CONSTRUCTION***\n\nThe map construction is at times so easy and so hard.\nBasically\
    \ it's really easy: you sample your sensor, transform the readings with the transformation\
    \ matrix pertaining to the robot and sensor poses ([Denavit-Hartemberg: kinematic\
    \ chains](http://www.cs.duke.edu/brd/Teaching/Bio/asmb/current/Papers/chap3-forward-kinematics.pdf))\
    \ and put the output of these operations on the map.\nSo far is the theory.\n\n\
    Reality has to cope with many different problems:\n\n - sensor imperfection: noise\
    \ on range readings, noise on angles, reflective surfaces, echos...\n - odometry\
    \ errors: integral errors on position and orientation\n\n\nThis often means that\
    \ you can't take those readings and transformations as gold and it's here that\
    \ a lot of filtering goes on.\n\nThe Kalman family of filters has one main feature:\
    \ it melds different sources of data with different uncertainties granting you\
    \ the best possible result of what you have in your hands.\n\nOn of the biggest\
    \ uses is to augment the positioning accuracy of the onboard sensors:\nattitude\
    \ is obtained by accelerometers augmented with gyroscopes (and eventually magnetometers),\
    \ position is obtained by odometry augmented with GPS.\nBut this kind of filter\
    \ can work with any flow of data, as Mac told you above it's a method and it's\
    \ really generic: you could even filter two maps with a Kalman, it's just up to\
    \ your implementation (and the significance of the covariance matrices).\n\nI\
    \ have digressed a bit, getting back to the point: the key factor in map building\
    \ is the accuracy of the absolute positioning of the robot with respect to the\
    \ environment.\nEssentially how sure you are of where you are in relation with\
    \ the world.\nExcept that if you don't know the world, as you are mapping it,\
    \ how would you know precisely where you are in it?\n\n\n***LOCALIZATION***\n\n\
    Localization is just what the name says: determining the location of the robot\
    \ in relation to the external world.\nThere are many methods and these strictly\
    \ depends on the kind of structure you use for your experiment (structured world,\
    \ unstructured world, heavily/lightly structured world...).\n\nEven the same lab\
    \ may be heavily or lightly structured or even unstructured.\n\n - Structured:\
    \ you have (lots of) external (relative to the robot) means to augment some/any/all\
    \ robot senses\n - Unstructured: the robot depends exclusively on the internal\
    \ sensors it has\n\nFor example physical landmarks are external localization aids:\
    \ thus if you use physical landmarks (coloured patches on the ground or on the\
    \ walls, rfids, wifi networks... whatever) you are in a structured environment.\n\
    Conversely if you robot runs free in the woods it is an unstructured environment.\n\
    \nI already did something confusing: I talked about landmarks, but a landmark\
    \ is something you recognize on the map, so weren't I talking about LOCALIZATION\
    \ and *not* MAPPING?\n\nYes but they are closely intertwined.\nYou can't localize\
    \ without a map and you can't map without a location.\n\nThat's why all the SLAM\
    \ family of algorithms is so cool nowadays (and because we have the computing\
    \ power to run them). SLAM stands for *Simultaneous Localization And Mapping*.\n\
    \nSince you have to do both, it's stupid doing this in two separate and mutually\
    \ unaware algorithms: let's use a single big one that can take advantage of knowing\
    \ both sides.\nSo you run your scans and confront the readings with the actual\
    \ map: some data is going to help you understand where you are, some other is\
    \ going to add to the current map.\n\nThis kind of localization though is fairly\
    \ advanced and is heavily based on statistics (most of it Bayesian) and thus statistical\
    \ filters (particle filters for example). Theoretically a Kalman could do something\
    \ for SLAM (as Kalman is a statistical filter too) but the performance would be\
    \ much worse then a dedicated filter: as  said above Kalman can fuse two streams,\
    \ while particle filters can work with a limitless number of particles thus allowing\
    \ for a much finer precision in the end result.\nHave a look at www.openslam.org\
    \ for the freely available SLAM algorithms.\n\n\nSo SLAMs are used in very unstructured\
    \ environments, where localizations is quite hard, so if you want to start easy\
    \ on experimenting, it would be better to use a heavily structured environment\
    \ (external positioning, like GPS, or cameras or... you name it) and thus alleviating\
    \ at least one of the problems.\n\n\n\n\n\nNow this is but a generic introduction\
    \ to the range of problems you're going to meet in Robotics, it's the proverbial\
    \ can of worms: I have barely moved the lid.\n\nOpening it is up to you.\nGood\
    \ luck!\n\n\n***ADDENDUM***\nI just noticed you highlighted a part of your question.\n\
    Regarding the Kalman family, it should all be clear.\nRegarding the Particle Filters\
    \ family I don't have that much experience, but as far as I know the DP-SLAM algorithm\
    \ for example (freely available on the openslam site) doesn't use features.\n\n\
    Features are quite hard to manage, and are often left for 3D positioning (see\
    \ pointcloud library at *www.pointclouds.org*): if you are doing 2D then features\
    \ are most often used as landmarks, but this again depends on the structure.\n\
    Doing landmark recognition in unstructured environments is something still only\
    \ for humans (or thereabouts): robots still find it quite hard to recognize the\
    \ Tour Eiffel, Reichstag, Space Needle or... :P\n\nIf you really intend to explore\
    \ feature recognition then you have to experiment with the representation. For\
    \ example in office buildings corners appear as triangles in an XY plot where\
    \ X is angle and Y is distance as read from the sensor; while doors are either\
    \ scarcely visible (when closed) or rectangular discontinuities.\nBut this is\
    \ heavily related to the environment being indoor offices, if you move to the\
    \ outside of a campus who knows what you can find? (well MIT knows for sure!)"
