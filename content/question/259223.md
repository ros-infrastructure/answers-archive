  body_markdown: "There are at least three popular ways to specify a rotation in 3-D.\n\
    \n* 3x3 rotation matrix (or 4x4 matrix in homogeneous co\xF6rdinates)\n* Euler\
    \ angles (such as angles of roll, pitch, and yaw, often X, Y, and Z axis rotations,\
    \ though there are many variations on Euler angles)\n* Quaternions (specifies\
    \ an axis of rotation and an angle of rotation about that axis)\n\nThe main thing\
    \ to know about a quaternion is that it encodes two things in a 4-tuple: 1) an\
    \ axis of rotation (a 3-D vector), and 2) an angle of rotation, measured as the\
    \ counter-clockwise rotation in radians about that vector (right-hand rule). This\
    \ can represent any 3-D rotation using four elements, only slightly more memory\
    \ than used by Euler angles, and considerably less than the matrix representation.\n\
    \nThe 4-tuple quaternion mixes the axis of rotation and angle in a strange way.\
    \ Let's say the axis of rotation is the vector `<x, y, z>` and the angle of rotation\
    \ is `theta`, measured as the right-hand rotation in radians about `<x, y, z>`.\
    \ Then the quaternion that represents that rotation is:\n\n    <cos(theta/2),\
    \ x*sin(theta/2), y*sin(theta/2), z*sin(theta/2)>\n\nThat's really all there is\
    \ to it, it's just a fairly compact representation of an arbitrary rotation. Quaternions\
    \ have several advantages over Euler angles and matrix representations.\n\n* More\
    \ compact than matrix representations.\n* Can perform rotations with fewer multiplications,\
    \ and mathematically more stable.\n* Can interpolate rotations component-by-component\
    \ to generate intermediate states smoothly.\n* No \"gimbal lock\" problem. (Google\
    \ this.)\n\n(Note: There are actually multiple forms of quaternion representations,\
    \ but only one in ROS.)"
