    \ is right. Indeed, that was what I meant.\n\n\nOne comment: if the sensor on\
    \ the base is too far from the floor, it may not work, or you will need lenses\
    \ to focus/compensate the distance. \n\nThis also reduces the hight of your base\
    \ and the possibility of going over obstacles.\n\nBut it is cheap, and tends to\
    \ be precise: optical mouses can work with resolution of typically 300 dpi.\n\n\
    \nQuick explanation of sketch: gray wheel: free wheel. Black wheel (yellow core)\
    \ drive wheels. \"Robot\" is mounted on a triangular base. \n\n![image description](https://ftp.osuosl.org/pub/ros/download.ros.org/downloads/se_migration/ros/14044198658898788.png)\n\
    \n\n\nOk, so let's think about the possibilities on what kind of data you can\
    \ gather from this.\n\nThe concept is simple physics: distance over time.\n\n\
    \ 1. If the wheel sensor reads\n    movement, you can actually calculate\n   \
    \ the linear speed of the wheel. \n\n2) If the linear speed of the wheel and\n\
    \    the linear speed detected by the\n    base sensor are the same, you robot\n\
    \    is moving. \n\n3) If the linear speed of\n    the wheel is greater than the\
    \ linear\n    speed of the \"floor\", your drive\n    wheel is skidding.  \n\n\
    4) If the linear\n    speed of the floor is greater than\n    the linear speed\
    \ of the wheel, your\n    robot is being dragged or going down\n    a very slippery\
    \ path.  \n\n5) if the\n    base sensor detects movement in the\n    X and Y axes,\
    \ your robot is either\n    making a curve, or spinning. You can\n    calculate\
    \ angular speed of the\n    curve, if you think you need it. \n\n6) If you detect\
    \ that the wheel speed\n    is fluctuating, your robot may be on\n    some sort\
    \ of low cohesion soil, like\n    pebbles.\n\n "
